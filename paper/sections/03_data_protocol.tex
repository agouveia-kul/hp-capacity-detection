\section{Data and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:data}

In this section, the source datasets and the two datasets built from them are described first. The construction of the semi-synthetic aggregates and the evaluation protocol follow.

\subsection{Source Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}

The source datasets used in this paper are listed in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}. Two of them provide submetered HP electricity, and two provide the load of households without HPs.

The HEAPO dataset contains smart meter data of \num{1408} households with HPs in the canton of Zurich~\cite{brudermueller2025heapo}. It was recorded at 15-minute and daily resolution between 2018 and 2024, and a subset of the households has a separate submeter for the HP. Weather data from eight stations are included.

The dataset of Kaiser et al.\ contains 15-minute smart meter data of \num{2447} residential installations in Switzerland for 2023 and 2024~\cite{kaiser2026swiss}. Its metadata flag the major loads of each installation, such as HPs, backup heating rods, electric water heaters and storage heaters. Some HPs are measured by their own meter, next to the meter of the dwelling. The dataset has no weather data, so the MeteoSwiss station of Zurich-Kloten, the closest station to these installations, is used~\cite{kaiser2026swiss}.

The Electrification of Heat (EoH) Demonstration Project installed and monitored HPs in 742 homes in South East England, North East England and South East Scotland~\cite{esc2024eoh}. Its cleansed 30-minute dataset covers November 2020 to September 2023. The EoH data measure the electricity of the whole heating system, which includes the compressor, the backup heater, the immersion heater for hot water and the circulation pumps. The homes are grouped into weather groups, which share one outdoor temperature series. These groups are used as weather stations in this paper.

The Low Carbon London (LCL) trial recorded 30-minute smart meter data of London households~\cite{schofield2015lcl}. Part of these households were placed on a dynamic time-of-use tariff in 2013, while the others stayed on a flat-rate tariff. The LCL data contain no information on HPs. Outdoor temperature for these households is the daily mean at London Heathrow, obtained from Meteostat~\cite{meteostat}.

\label{sec:chgb}

\subsubsection{\dataCH}
Calendar year 2023 is used as the study period, since it is the only year covered by both Swiss datasets. A meter is kept only if at least 90\% of its raw 15-minute samples in that year are valid. This rule is checked before any gap is filled, so that interpolated values cannot inflate the coverage. Remaining gaps are then interpolated linearly in time.

Three types of HP household pass this rule. HEAPO contributes 47 households with an HP submeter and a channel for the remaining household load. The Kaiser et al.\ dataset contributes 24 dwellings whose meter is paired with a separate HP meter. It also contributes 15 HP meters in single-family houses that have no dwelling meter. Each of these 15 HP meters is given the load of one unused single-family dwelling without HP, drawn at random with a fixed seed. In total, the \dataCH{} has 86 HP households spread over five weather stations.

The fillers are the \num{1291} dwellings of the Kaiser et al.\ dataset whose metadata flag no Thermostatically Controlled Load (TCL). These dwellings have no HP, electric water heater, storage heater or direct electric heating. It should be noted that the own non-HP load of an HP household is kept as measured, and can include an electric water heater.

A smaller sensitivity subset keeps only the 47 HP households at the Zurich-Kloten station. % TODO: confirm whether this subset (repo name B) is reported in the paper (DECISIONS 2026-09-29: sensitivity arm).

\subsubsection{\dataGB}
A home of the EoH project is eligible if at least 90\% of its 30-minute bins are valid within a 12-month window. Gaps of up to two hours are interpolated, while longer gaps are filled with a donor day from the same home and day type. Runs of zero electricity of six hours or more are treated as missing only when the heat meter still records heat output, which indicates a meter dropout. Runs without heat output are kept as real switch-offs. Hybrid systems are excluded, since their boiler data are unreliable.

The 12-month window starts in the month that gives the most eligible homes. Among the start months from June 2021 to January 2022, this is November 2021, so the main window runs from November 2021 to October 2022. One home is excluded because its electricity reads close to zero on 15\% of the bins in which heat is delivered. This pattern points to a silent electricity meter rather than to real behaviour.

Weather groups with more than 5\% missing temperature are filled from the best-correlated group if that correlation is at least 0.98, and are dropped with their homes otherwise. The resulting \dataGB{} has 384 homes in 26 groups. Of these, 217 have an air-source HP, 153 a high-temperature air-source HP and 14 a ground-source HP.

A second window, from October 2022 to 28 September 2023, serves as a temporal replication. The end date is the last full day of EoH data. It contains 319 homes, of which 272 are also in the main window. It is therefore a second year of mostly the same homes, not an independent sample.

The EoH data contain no whole-house meter, so the fillers of the \dataGB{} come from the LCL trial. Only households on the flat-rate tariff are used, since the price signals of the dynamic tariff shift load in time. A household is kept if at least 90\% of its data are valid between July 2012 and February 2014. Households with any half-hour above 10~kWh, or with a run of zeros of 24 hours or more, are also removed. Of \num{4173} flat-rate households, \num{3199} remain.

The LCL data were recorded about nine years before the EoH data, so they are not used on their own calendar days. Instead, each filler is mapped to the EoH days by matching days with similar temperature and the same day type, as described in Section~\ref{sec:aggregates}.

\subsubsection{Labels per dataset}
The HP label of a household is computed from its HP channel, which differs between the two datasets. In the \dataCH{} the channel is the HP submeter only, whereas in the \dataGB{} it is the whole heating system, including the immersion heater. Labels are also computed at different resolutions, 15 minutes and 30 minutes respectively. For these reasons, results are compared between the two datasets through their gap to a physics-based estimator, and not through absolute errors.

\subsection{Semi-Synthetic Aggregates}
\label{sec:aggregates}

Feeders with submetered HPs and a known HP capacity are rarely available in numbers large enough for supervised learning. For this reason, semi-synthetic aggregates are built by summing the measured loads of distinct households, as summarised in Fig.~\ref{fig:design}. Each aggregate stands for the net load of the dwellings behind one LV feeder. Network losses are not modelled, since the aim is a realistic aggregate rather than an exact copy of a real feeder.

\subsubsection{Splits before construction}
Households are split into a training set and a test set before any aggregate is built. In each weather station, one quarter of the HP households is drawn for the test set. The fillers are split in the same proportion over the whole dataset, independently of the HP households. Training aggregates are then built from training households only and test aggregates from test households only, so that no household appears in both sets. Earlier versions of this benchmark did not follow this rule, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:pitfalls}. The number of splits and the folds used for tuning are described in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.

\subsubsection{Composition of an aggregate}
An aggregate is defined by its weather station, its size $N$ in dwellings and its HP penetration $p$, which give it $n=\max\bigl(1,\operatorname{round}(pN)\bigr)$ HP households. All HP households of an aggregate are drawn from one weather station, so that one temperature series drives its whole heating load. The same can be expected on a feeder that serves a small area. Every dwelling of an aggregate is a distinct household, and HP households are never used as fillers. All draws are made without replacement and with a fixed seed per split.

In the \dataCH, an aggregate contains $N-n$ fillers and $n$ HP households, each with its HP load and its own non-HP load as measured. In the \dataGB, the EoH homes have no record of their non-HP load, so each of the $N$ dwellings receives one filler, HP dwellings included. The net load of aggregate $a$ is thus
\begin{equation}
P^{\mathrm{net}}_a(t) = \sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_a} \bigl(h_i(t) + o_i(t)\bigr) + \sum_{j\in\mathcal{F}_a} f_j(t),
\label{eq:aggregate}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{H}_a$ and $\mathcal{F}_a$ are the HP households and the fillers of the aggregate. The HP load is denoted by $h_i$, the own non-HP load by $o_i$ and the filler load by $f_j$. In the \dataGB, $o_i$ is zero and $f_j$ is taken on analog days, as described in Section~\ref{sec:analog}.

\subsubsection{Grid and design envelope}
The grid has sizes $N$ of 10, 20, 40, 80 and 120 dwellings and penetrations $p$ of 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 0.8 and 1.0. A grid cell is one combination of station, size and penetration within one set. A cell is kept only if $n \le 0.75H$, where $H$ is the number of HP households of that station in the set. This bound keeps the aggregates of one cell from sharing most of their HP households. Stations with fewer than three HP households in a set are not used. Cells outside these bounds are dropped and listed, and no aggregate is truncated to fit. Each kept cell gives ten training aggregates or five test aggregates.

The resulting design envelope is given in Table~\ref{tab:envelope}, and its counts are the same in every split since the split is stratified by station. Since the aggregates of one set reuse the same households many times, the number of distinct HP households is the effective sample size of each result. The \dataCH{} gives ten test aggregates in each of the two upper penetration bins. The \dataGB{} gives at least 30 test aggregates in every bin.

\subsubsection{Fillers for the \dataGB{} from analog days}
\label{sec:analog}
The LCL fillers were recorded in 2012--2014 and the EoH homes in 2021--2023, so their calendar days cannot be aligned directly. Instead, every EoH day is paired with an LCL day of similar weather, called its analog day.

For each weather group $g$ and EoH day $d$, the analog day $d'$ is chosen from the LCL days in four steps.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The candidate has the same day type as $d$ in the England and Wales calendar, i.e.\ a working day or a weekend or bank holiday.
\item Its day of the year lies within 30 days of $d$. This window is widened to 45 and then 60 days only when no candidate passes the next step.
\item Its daily mean temperature at Heathrow lies within 1~K of that of group $g$ on day $d$.
\item One of the three candidates closest in temperature is drawn at random with a fixed seed.
\end{enumerate}
If no candidate passes the third step even at 60 days, the candidate closest in temperature is taken. The map from $d$ to $d'$ is fixed per weather group and split. All fillers of an aggregate take the same analog day, which possibly preserves their common response to the weather and the calendar of that day. The half-hours of $d'$ are then placed on the local clock time of $d$.\footnote{Days on which daylight saving time changes are not candidates. A target day of 23 hours skips the missing half-hours, and a target day of 25 hours uses the repeated ones twice.}

This construction assumes that the load of a filler is independent of the HP load, given temperature, day type and time of year. This is the conditional independence assumption of statistical matching, which cannot be tested with the two datasets alone~\cite{dorazio2006statistical}. Its consequences are therefore checked by the diagnostics below. The choice of whole days by nearest neighbours within a seasonal window follows the nearest-neighbour resampling used in weather generators~\cite{lall1996nearest,rajagopalan1999knn}. It is also close to the analog method of statistical downscaling~\cite{zorita1999analog} and to similar-day methods in load forecasting~\cite{dudek2015pattern}.

Measured HP profiles have been added to national electricity demand before~\cite{love2017addition}. Household smart meter data have also been combined with EoH HP data into synthetic household profiles~\cite{agrawal2026electricity}. To the best of the authors' knowledge, no previous work pairs separately measured HP and household loads from different years on temperature-analog days.

Five diagnostics, D1 to D5, test the analog-day fillers. They are listed in Table~\ref{tab:diag}, and each flag threshold was fixed before the run. D1 raised no flag, since the heating slope of the filler aggregate changes by 5.9\% when it is moved onto the EoH days. At two of the 16 stations, however, this change exceeds 15\%. D2 raised no flag either, since only 0.24\% of the EoH days are matched with a temperature difference above 1~K.

D4 rebuilds one LCL winter from the other LCL days with the same rule, and compares it with the real winter. The median peak of the rebuilt filler aggregates differs from the real one by 3\% to 7\%. Their heating slope differs by far more than the threshold, so D4 raised its flag. A second rule, also fixed before any run, then tested whether this error comes from the mapping or from the fillers themselves. It compares the error with the real change in slope of the same filler aggregates between the winters 2012/13 and 2013/14. This real change is 52\% to 78\% in median, depending on the aggregate size. It is of the same size as the rebuild error, which ranges from 38\% to 94\%. The rule therefore classifies the error as intrinsic filler variability, and the mapping is kept.

This variability of real households limits any estimator that reads the HP capacity from the heating slope of the net load. Its effect is expressed as a capacity-equivalent error, i.e.\ the slope error divided by the slope per kW of HP peak of the training HP households. This error exceeds 5\% of $P^{\mathrm{HP}}$ at every penetration up to 0.5, for at least one aggregate size. These penetrations cover the three lower penetration bins of Paper A~\cite{paperA} and are marked as filler-variability-limited in every GB table. The mark is a floor for any estimator, not a defect of the \dataGB. The test uses winter slopes, whereas the estimators fit a full year, so this floor is possibly pessimistic. A sensitivity analysis also scales the temperature response of the fillers by 0.5 and 1.5.

D5 applies the same rule to the \dataCH, in which the HP load and the own non-HP load of each household were measured together. Each dwelling receives an analog-day filler from the same year.\footnote{Candidate days within three days of the target day are excluded, and each aggregate is matched on the temperature of its own station.} The estimators are then scored on these aggregates and on the real ones. D5 thus measures the cost of the analog-day construction, including the loss of each household's own non-HP load. It raised its flag for some estimators and penetration bins, so its differences are reported next to every headline GB result in Section~\ref{sec:rq2}.

The analog-day fillers cannot reproduce every property of a real feeder. First, the own non-HP load of an HP home is replaced by that of an unrelated household, so any link between the two is lost. Second, the LCL loads reflect the appliances and occupancy of 2012--2014. Third, only the daily mean temperature is matched, so solar gain, wind and humidity are not. Fourth, consecutive EoH days take unrelated LCL days, so multi-day cold spells are not preserved in the fillers. Finally, all flat-rate households are kept, so electrically heated LCL homes add to the temperature response of the fillers.
